# Tutor session notes: how SI / peer-tutoring programmes use them

Research date: 2026-10-19. Purpose: inform a redesign of the Emeris tutor Notes page (per session claim).
Scope: Supplemental Instruction (SI, UMKC model), PASS (UK SI variant), CRLA certification, writing/tutoring centre session reports, and the published research on session notes.

## Summary

- **In SI, the main written artefact is a plan made before the session, not a log written after it.** The UMKC-model *Leader's Guide* tells leaders to "prepare a written session plan for each SI session" and to show it to the supervisor and the instructor. The plan covers learning objectives, the most difficult content, activities, and checks for understanding ([Leader's Guide to SI, pp. 8, 13, 54-59](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)).
- **Lecturers receive student difficulties, not session transcripts.** SI leaders "support the classroom instructor by providing feedback about students' difficulties, content-related issues". UK PASS schemes send this feedback through a weekly debrief, where "troublesome topics are reported to the teaching team" ([Leader's Guide p. 8](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf); [UCLan via BCS](https://www.bcs.org/deliver-and-teach-qualifications/academic-accreditation/practice-highlights/university-of-central-lancashire-peer-assisted-study-sessions-pass/); [Ody & Carey, Manchester](https://documents.manchester.ac.uk/display.aspx?DocID=7418)).
- **Anonymity is a norm in SI.** Leaders are told to "respect the anonymity of the student participants", and some programmes do not tell faculty which students attend. What lecturers get is aggregate content feedback ([Leader's Guide p. 13](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf); [UVM SI faculty resources](https://www.uvm.edu/academicsuccess/undergraduate-tutoring-center/si-faculty-resources)).
- **Most tutors treat post-session reports as busywork when nobody reads them.** In a 2026 study, most tutors saw reports as "routine administrative tasks rather than meaningful tools for reflection or feedback". Two-thirds spent under 5 minutes per report, most rarely re-read earlier reports, and none shared reports with faculty ([Salmenbayeva 2026, *Young Scholars in Writing* 23](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)).
- **Notes are useful when they support the student's next step.** Students, faculty and tutors all agreed that "the chief purpose for tutor notes should be supporting student progress". A balanced summary plus goals ("Reporter Note") was the favourite; encouragement-only "Cheerleader" notes were judged not useful (Bugdal, Reardon & Deans 2016, *Writing Center Journal* 35(3), as reported in [Salmenbayeva 2026](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)).
- **Good note formats are short and structured, written to the student's work.** Rochester asks for a 5-7 minute, three-part summary: what the student brought and wanted, what was done, and the next step. Tutors are told to comment "on the work, not the student" ([Rochester Writing & Speaking Center](https://writing.rochester.edu/tutoring/sessions.html)).
- **Session records often double as proof for pay.** One college submits tutee-signed Progress Reports with timesheets "as proof that the tutee was present for the sessions billed" ([COTC](https://cotc.edu/post/tutor-resources)). CRLA counts "time sheets, schedule changes, session notes" as one administrative competency and requires logged tutoring hours for certification ([Fresno State quoting the CRLA standard](https://studentaffairs.fresnostate.edu/lrc/tutoring/tutortraining.html); [Kingsborough](https://kingsborough.edu/KLC/CRLA-Certification.html)).
- **Read in aggregate, notes become programme data.** Researchers have mined 1,261 to about 2 million words of notes to see what students struggle with and how training changes tutor practice ([Giaimo & Turner 2019, *J. Writing Research*](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/595); [Cheatle et al., *Kairos* 25.1](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/25.1/praxis/cheatle-et-al/methods.html)).

## Findings by theme

### 1. SI: plan before, feed back difficulties after

- The UMKC-model *Leader's Guide to Supplemental Instruction* (University of Missouri, 2024) lists these leader duties ([pp. 8-9](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)):
  - "Prepare a written session plan for each SI session."
  - "Show session plans to your Supervisor and the instructor for feedback (especially the first few weeks)."
  - "Include the learning objectives, difficult content, and strategies for the learning activities."
  - Collect attendance at every session and help the supervisor prepare the final report.
- The sample **SI Session Planning Form** ([pp. 54-58](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)) has these fields:
  - Session goal and opener.
  - Per activity: collaborative technique, learning strategy, time, materials, and lecture/textbook references.
  - Fully worked problems, then "Checking for Understanding (questions & answers)".
  - Closer.
  - A plan checklist that ends with "Plan is available for anyone to view".
- The first planning question is "What is the most difficult content? Remember, important is not the same as difficult." The guide also notes leaders often exceed "the paid planning time", so planning is paid work ([p. 59-60](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)).
- Closers are where difficulties get captured: ask students "what they are still struggling with so future sessions can be planned" ([p. 60](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)).
- The guide's instructions on working with faculty ([p. 13](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)):
  - "Provide the instructor with feedback about how the sessions are going."
  - "Show the instructor a copy of your plan."
  - Discourage the instructor from observing, to protect anonymity.
  - A training scenario covers an instructor asking "which students have been attending" and asking for "feedback about content related difficulties".
- UVM promises faculty an "end-of-term comparative analysis of student performance" and says "the faculty member will not be given the names of students who attend SI". Leaders give feedback "if requested" ([UVM](https://www.uvm.edu/academicsuccess/undergraduate-tutoring-center/si-faculty-resources)).

### 2. PASS (UK): debrief as the feedback channel

- At Manchester, weekly debriefs are meant to ensure "effective feedback to academic staff on common problems experienced by attendees and an insight into how the current material delivered within lectures, seminars and tutorials is being received" ([Ody & Carey](https://documents.manchester.ac.uk/display.aspx?DocID=7418)).
- At UCLan, "troublesome topics are reported to the teaching team, so they can be addressed in future classes" ([BCS practice highlight](https://www.bcs.org/deliver-and-teach-qualifications/academic-accreditation/practice-highlights/university-of-central-lancashire-peer-assisted-study-sessions-pass/)).
- At Hull, the academic contact joins at least the first 15 minutes of the debrief, described as "a great opportunity to gain weekly feedback from students on the current teaching" ([Hull PASS for academics](https://libguides.hull.ac.uk/pass/academics)).
- At Brighton, leaders spend 5 minutes after a session noting thoughts on an evaluation sheet and raise them at the next debrief. Attendance registers are "vital for the evaluation of PASS" ([Brighton PASS leader resources](https://blogs.brighton.ac.uk/pass/pass-leader-resources)).
- In PASS the written record is light: a register plus a short reflection. The difficulties travel to academics in aggregated form, through a coordinator or debrief.

### 3. Writing/tutoring-centre session reports: audiences and the sharing debate

- Whether to share notes with faculty is a long-running debate. Pemberton called the two sides "sharers" and "seclusionists" (1995). Cogie (1998) found that writing and sharing summaries strengthens student-tutor and student-teacher relationships (both via [Salmenbayeva 2026](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)).
- Weaver (2001) argued that tutor-only notes undercut student authority and proposed "jointly told tales", meaning notes co-written with the student (via [Salmenbayeva 2026](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)).
- Bugdal, Reardon & Deans (2016) studied note types (Reporter, Bro, Coach, Cheerleader, Quick) against what students, faculty and tutors found useful (via [Salmenbayeva 2026](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445); original: [WCJ 35(3)](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/wcj/vol35/iss3/3)):
  - The Reporter Note, which balances summary and goal-setting, was the favourite of all three groups.
  - Cheerleader notes were "not useful/purposeful in general".
- Malenczyk (2013) identifies informational, educational and community-building functions. Practitioners also report the downsides ([Zito 2016, UW-Madison *Another Word*](https://dept.writing.wisc.edu/blog/tutor-session-reports-narratives-we-build-together/)):
  - Records are written in a rush.
  - They can "flatten" students for the next tutor.
  - Tutors fear they document their own failures.
- Defaults vary by institution:
  - **Internal only:** Michigan's notes are "entirely internal to the writing center staff; they are not shared with writers or with instructors" ([Cheatle et al.](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/25.1/praxis/cheatle-et-al/methods.html)).
  - **Student copy and re-reading:** At Texas A&M, consultants review a client's prior notes before sessions, administrators review notes three times a semester, and clients request copies "as a reinforcement of the concepts covered" ([Cheatle et al.](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/25.1/praxis/cheatle-et-al/methods.html)).
  - **WCOnline:** Reports are "confidential and only available to administrators" by default. They can be emailed to "an advisor or instructor", and every send is logged ([WCOnline help](https://help.mywconline.com/data-collection-forms/client-report-forms/)).
  - **Student consent:** Trinity College tutors ask the student at session end whether they were required to attend and "would they want someone to get a copy of this form" ([Trinity SBTC WCOnline instructions](https://trincoll.edu/trinityplus/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/09/WCOnline-Instructions-for-SBTC-Tutors.pdf)).
- One study asked whether report forms actually convey useful information to instructors, whom it treated as the primary audience. It surveyed instructors and retrained consultants on the results ([Stainbrook & Rahman, ECWCA 2019](https://ecommons.udayton.edu/ecwca/ECWCA_2019/Fri_Day2/47)).

### 4. Evidence that notes become busywork

- Salmenbayeva (2026) studied about 200 reports plus a tutor survey at Nazarbayev University ([article](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)):
  - 66.7% of tutors spent under 5 minutes per report.
  - Only 1 of 9 "always" re-read prior reports before a returning student.
  - 66.7% called the task "redundant, burdensome, or distracting".
  - One tutor said: "No goals really. It's just a requirement."
  - None shared reports with faculty, and some did not know sharing with students was possible.
  - The author attributes this to a "lack of shared purpose", seen in how rarely reports are revisited or shared and in "minimal integration of this practice into the tutors' existing workflow".
- Category fields lose meaning. Students picked "organization" and "conciseness" from a dropdown, but what was actually worked on varied widely. "Conciseness" appeared in 41 booking forms but in only 12 reports that focused on it. The study suggests preset options "save them the time and effort necessary to reflect" ([same](https://youngscholarsinwriting.org/index.php/ysiw/article/view/445)).
- Training changes note quality. A study of 1,261 notes over six semesters found that "specific trainings can change the behavior of experienced tutors" ([Giaimo & Turner 2019](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/595)).

### 5. CRLA: documentation as a trained competency, hours as certification evidence

- The CRLA ITTPC Level 1 "Basics" category includes "Administrative Policies, Record Keeping and Reporting". The standard reads: "The tutor knows the administrative processes outlined by the tutorial program (i.e., time sheets, schedule changes, session notes, etc.)" ([Fresno State LRC, quoting the ITTPC standard](https://studentaffairs.fresnostate.edu/lrc/tutoring/tutortraining.html)).
- At Level 3, the Master Tutor "reflects critically on how training has influenced their work with students" ([same](https://studentaffairs.fresnostate.edu/lrc/tutoring/tutortraining.html)).
- A Level 1 application requires 10 hours of training and "at least 25 hours of actual tutoring experience per tutor" documented in a log, with redacted training logs and a sample trainee evaluation ([Kingsborough CRLA certification](https://kingsborough.edu/KLC/CRLA-Certification.html)). Session/hour logs are therefore also certification evidence.
- Caveat: CRLA's own requirements page ([crla.net/page/ittpcrequirements](https://crla.net/page/ittpcrequirements)) blocked automated fetch. The quotes above are institutions reproducing the standard.

### 6. Session records linked to pay and verification

- At COTC: "Every tutee must sign a Progress Report at the end of each tutoring session... submitted with Timesheets as proof that the tutee was present for the sessions billed" ([COTC tutor resources](https://cotc.edu/post/tutor-resources)).
- WCOnline ties each report to an appointment, records "actual appointment length" separately from the booked length, and has an "orphan appointments" listing for appointments with no report ([WCOnline help](https://help.mywconline.com/data-collection-forms/client-report-forms/); [Trinity](https://trincoll.edu/trinityplus/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2024/09/WCOnline-Instructions-for-SBTC-Tutors.pdf)). In effect, the missing note is the audit flag.
- SI pays leaders for planning time as well as session time ([Leader's Guide p. 60](https://si.mtsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2025/04/SI-Leader-Manual-.pdf)).
- Rochester states one purpose of summaries as "to keep track of how much the fellows' services are used" ([Rochester](https://writing.rochester.edu/tutoring/sessions.html)).

## Patterns: useful vs busywork

| Notes are useful when... | Notes become busywork when... |
|---|---|
| A named reader acts on them: the next tutor, the student, the coordinator, or the lecturer (SI, PASS, Texas A&M) | Nobody reads them; "it's just a requirement" (Salmenbayeva) |
| They feed forward: next steps, and what to plan next session (Rochester conclusion; SI closer) | They only look back at what happened, with no consequence |
| Difficulties are aggregated across sessions and passed to teaching staff (PASS debriefs) | Each note stays isolated in a database |
| They are short and structured, about 5-7 minutes, in 3 parts (Rochester) | They duplicate fields already known (date, module, attendance) or repeat the plan |
| They are concrete, about the work: page/problem references, worked examples (SI form) | They are vague category picks or encouragement only ("Cheerleader"; dropdown "organization") |
| They are surfaced in the tutor's workflow, e.g. prior notes before the next session | Tutors must go looking for them; few ever re-read (1 of 9 "always") |
| They respect anonymity: content feedback to lecturers, not who struggled (SI) | Tutors fear the note is surveillance of them or of students (Zito) |
| Training shows what a good note looks like (Giaimo & Turner; CRLA Level 1) | There is no guidance and no feedback on notes |

## Implications for Emeris

1. **Give every field a reader.** Map each field to who uses it and drop fields that have none.
   - `student_struggles` and `revision_topics` go to the lecturer.
   - Next steps go to the tutor's next session.
   - Coverage goes to claim verification.
   - The standalone "coverage confirmed" checkbox should either become part of claim submission or be removed.
2. **Split the page into "Plan" (before) and "Debrief" (after).**
   - Plan follows the SI model: goal, most difficult content, activities, check-for-understanding questions, optionally shared with the lecturer.
   - Debrief follows the Rochester model, three short prompts: worked on, where students got stuck, next step.
   - The debrief should take about 5 minutes.
3. **Prefill from data Emeris already has** (module, schedule, attendance count, the previous session's next steps) so tutors only write what is new. Do not re-ask what the claim already records.
4. **Build a lecturer-facing "Student difficulties" digest per module/week.**
   - Aggregate `student_struggles` across tutors and sessions. This is the PASS "troublesome topics to the teaching team" loop.
   - Keep it anonymous: content only, no student names.
   - This is the strongest reason for tutors to write good struggle notes. Let lecturers acknowledge or respond, so tutors can see the notes were read.
5. **Feed forward to the tutor.** On the next session's plan, show the last session's "next step" and "still struggling" items, so notes are re-used without the tutor having to look for them.
6. **Link notes to claims lightly.** Require a minimal debrief (for example, topics covered plus one struggle or "none") before `submitClaimForVerification`. This mirrors the COTC and WCOnline practice of treating the session record as evidence for billed time. Lecturers then verify against it. Keep the bar low so the requirement does not turn into busywork.
7. **Prefer specific free text over category pickers.** Use chips or tags only for aggregation, alongside a required concrete example (problem number, slide, misconception). Dropdown-only categories lost meaning in the Nazarbayev data.
8. **Repurpose the AI placeholders toward aggregation:**
   - Cluster struggles across sessions for the lecturer digest.
   - Draft the debrief from the plan for the tutor to edit.
   - Turn "concept explainer" into "suggest a check-for-understanding question for next session".
   - Avoid AI that writes a narrative nobody reads.
9. **Add an in-app "what a good note looks like" example** (Reporter-style: summary plus goal, about the work not the student). This covers the CRLA "session notes" administrative competency.
